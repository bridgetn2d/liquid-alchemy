ICONIC BARS & THEIR COCKTAILS
A Liquid Alchemy Research Summary
================================================================================

This document maps the world's most storied bars to the drinks they invented,
with notes on lore potential and Alchemy Standard relevance. Organized by tier:
historically iconic first, contemporary legends second, with special sections
on New Orleans and the Japanese influence thread.

================================================================================
HISTORICALLY ICONIC
================================================================================

EL FLORIDITA — Havana, Cuba (est. ~1817)
Signature drinks: Daiquiri, Hemingway Daiquiri
"The cradle of the Daiquiri." Hemingway's favorite bar — he is immortalized in
bronze at his usual stool. The Hemingway Daiquiri (no sugar, double rum,
grapefruit juice, maraschino) was created to his personal specification. The
original Floridita Daiquiri — Havana Club rum, lemon juice, maraschino, sugar —
maintains its own celebrated status alongside the Hemingway variant.
Lore rating: Essential. Two attributable recipes, Hemingway connection, Cuban
cocktail history.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

LA BODEGUITA DEL MEDIO — Havana, Cuba (est. 1942)
Signature drink: Mojito
The other half of Hemingway's Havana drinking life. He was quoted: "My mojito
in La Bodeguita, my daiquiri in El Floridita," forever linking the bar with the
Mojito in global popular culture. The walls are covered floor to ceiling in
signatures and scrawlings from generations of visitors.
Note: The Mojito predates the bar; the attribution is by legend and endorsement,
not invention. Still strong lore material.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

HARRY'S BAR — Venice, Italy (est. 1931)
Signature drinks: Bellini, Martini
Giuseppe Cipriani invented the Bellini — Prosecco and white peach purée — in
1948. Hemingway, Orson Welles, and Truman Capote all drank here. Now a UNESCO
cultural landmark. The Bellini has clean attribution: one man, one bar, one year.
Lore rating: High. Bellini is a direct Alchemy Standard candidate.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

HARRY'S NEW YORK BAR — Paris, France (est. 1911)
Signature drinks: Bloody Mary, Sidecar, French 75
Founded 1911; favorite of Hemingway, Rita Hayworth, Jean-Paul Sartre. Claims
credit for inventing the Bloody Mary, the Sidecar, and the French 75. The
interior was shipped plank-by-plank from a Manhattan bar and reassembled in
Paris. All three attributions are contested but the claim is strong and
well-documented.
Lore rating: High. Three possible recipes, all with great stories.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

BAR HEMINGWAY, RITZ PARIS — Paris, France (est. 1921)
Signature drinks: Serendipity, Classic Martini
The intimate bar is adorned with leather armchairs, vintage photographs, and
literary memorabilia. The Serendipity — Calvados, apple juice, mint, Champagne
— was created by legendary head bartender Colin Field. Hemingway legendarily
"liberated" the bar himself in 1944 when Allied forces entered Paris, reportedly
arriving ahead of the troops to secure the premises.
Lore rating: Medium-high. Serendipity is a lesser-known recipe with strong lore.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

DUKES BAR — London, UK (est. 1908)
Signature drinks: Dukes Martini, Vesper
The house Martini was invented in 1987 by bartender Salvatore Calabrese. Poured
tableside from a frozen bottle — undiluted, brutally cold — guests are limited
to two. Ian Fleming was a regular; the Vesper (Bond's Martini) is closely
associated with the bar. The Martini here is not a recipe so much as a
performance.
Lore rating: High. Bond connection, theatrical service ritual, tableside prep.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

AMERICAN BAR, THE SAVOY — London, UK (est. 1889)
Signature drinks: Hanky Panky, White Lady, various Craddock classics
The oldest surviving cocktail bar in London. Two figures define its legacy:
Ada "Coley" Coleman became head bartender in 1903 — the first and only woman
to hold that position — and served 100,000 customers over 23 years. She invented
the Hanky Panky: gin, sweet vermouth, Fernet-Branca, orange peel. When actor
Sir Charles Hawtrey asked for something with "a bit of punch" and drained the
glass, he allegedly exclaimed: "By Jove! That is the real hanky-panky." Coleman
was quietly pushed out in 1925 and replaced by Harry Craddock, who went on to
write The Savoy Cocktail Book (1930) — and credited exactly one drink to her by
name: the Hanky Panky. Craddock also created the White Lady and corpse reviver
series. Named World's Best Bar in 2017.
Lore rating: Essential. Ada Coleman's story is one of the best in the canon —
maps directly onto the Singapore Sling ethos. Women doing extraordinary work in
spaces that didn't want them.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

LONG BAR, RAFFLES HOTEL — Singapore (est. ~1887, bar est. ~1899–1915)
Signature drink: Singapore Sling
Created by Hainanese-Chinese bartender Ngiam Tong Boon sometime between 1899
and 1915. The drink was conceived as a workaround — in colonial Singapore, women
were not permitted to consume alcohol in public, but fruit juice was perfectly
acceptable. The Sling's pink color and tropical sweetness gave women plausible
deniability, a cocktail disguised as something respectable. Ngiam Tong Boon
built the subversion right into the recipe. The original recipe was never
formally recorded; the closest thing to a primary source is a recipe scrawled on
a bar chit in 1936. Japanese forces occupied Raffles in 1942 and whatever records
existed were lost. The version served today is reconstructed from memory and that
lone 1936 note. Peanut shells on the floor remain an honored tradition.
Lore rating: Essential. Already in the library with upgraded lore.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

CAROUSEL BAR, HOTEL MONTELEONE — New Orleans, USA (est. 1886 hotel, bar 1949)
Signature drink: Vieux Carré
The bar rotates on 2,000 large steel rollers, completing a full turn every
fifteen minutes, powered by a quarter-horsepower motor — continuously since 1949.
The Vieux Carré (French for "Old Square," the name for the French Quarter) was
created by head bartender Walter Bergeron in the 1930s. Rye whiskey and cognac
as co-equal base spirits — American whiskey and French brandy sharing a glass,
neither yielding to the other, mirroring the city itself. United by sweet
vermouth, Bénédictine, Peychaud's and Angostura bitters. Tennessee Williams,
William Faulkner, and Truman Capote all drank here. Capote claimed to have been
born here — not true, but he repeated it often enough that the hotel eventually
put up a plaque.
Lore rating: Essential. Updated lore drafted in this session.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

ARNAUD'S FRENCH 75 BAR — New Orleans, USA (est. 1918, current bar 2003)
Signature drink: French 75 (New Orleans style — Cognac, not gin)
Nestled within Arnaud's Restaurant in the French Quarter. The bar was originally
a "gentlemen only" space; women were categorically excluded until renovations in
1979. Head bartender Chris Hannah has shaken more French 75s than arguably
anyone else in the world. The New Orleans version uses Cognac rather than gin —
a significant and debated departure from the classic recipe. James Beard Award
for Outstanding Bar Program, 2017.
Lore rating: High. French 75 has strong lore; the Cognac vs. gin debate is
genuinely interesting and very New Orleans.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

SAZERAC BAR, ROOSEVELT HOTEL — New Orleans, USA (est. 1938 at Roosevelt)
Signature drinks: Sazerac, Ramos Gin Fizz
The spiritual home of America's oldest cocktail. The Sazerac traces back to
1838 in the French Quarter; the Roosevelt Bar codified it for the modern era.
Also the canonical home of the Ramos Gin Fizz — invented by Henry Ramos in 1888,
notoriously requiring a 12-minute shake. During Carnival 1915, Ramos employed
over 30 bartenders to keep up with demand. The bar famously excluded women until
a group of them forced their way in — the "storming of the Sazerac."
Lore rating: High for both drinks. Sazerac is likely already in library;
Ramos Gin Fizz deserves attention.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

TRADER VIC'S — Oakland, CA (est. 1934, now global)
Signature drink: Mai Tai (original 1944 recipe)
As a treat for Tahitian friends in 1944, Vic Bergeron took a bottle of J. Wray
& Nephew 17-year rum from the back bar and mixed it with orgeat, lime juice,
rock candy syrup, and dry Curaçao. His guest exclaimed in Tahitian "Maita'i Roa
A'e!" — Out of this world, the best. The original rum went out of production;
Vic burned through his entire stock. Everything that followed — the sweet,
juice-heavy beach bar Mai Tai — is a degraded copy. The original is a
rum-forward, barely sweet cocktail of genuine elegance. Martin Cate of
Smuggler's Cove has spent decades restoring its reputation.
Lore rating: Essential for Tiki coverage. The degradation story is as important
as the origin.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

DON THE BEACHCOMBER — Hollywood, CA (est. 1934)
Signature drinks: Zombie, Rum Barrel, Missionary's Downfall
Donn Beach essentially invented Tiki culture here — Polynesian escapism through
rum, exotic fruit, and theatrical presentation. The Zombie was famously limited
to two per customer on account of its potency (three rums including overproof,
fruit juices, Falernum, absinthe rinse). Donn kept his recipes in coded form to
prevent staff from replicating them elsewhere. Cocktail historian Jeff "Beachbum"
Berry spent decades decoding them. Friendly rivals with Trader Vic's for Mai Tai
credit.
Lore rating: High. The coded recipes story is extraordinary. Essential for Tiki.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

BUENA VISTA CAFÉ — San Francisco, CA (est. 1916)
Signature drink: Irish Coffee (American introduction)
The Irish Coffee was invented at Shannon Airport in Ireland to warm passengers
after a grueling winter flight. Co-owner Jack Koeppler and San Francisco
Chronicle travel writer Stanton Delaplane brought it to America in the early
1950s after Delaplane tasted it at the airport. It took Koeppler months to
perfect the cream float. The Buena Vista now assembles them in a row of glasses
on the bar like a production line. One of the rare tourist pilgrimages that
fully delivers.
Lore rating: Medium-high. Irish Coffee is in most libraries; the Shannon Airport
origin and Delaplane backstory add real depth.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

TOMMY'S MEXICAN RESTAURANT — San Francisco, CA (est. 1965)
Signature drink: Tommy's Margarita
Not a cocktail bar in the conventional sense — a Yucatecan family restaurant in
the Richmond District. Julio Bermejo (son of founders Tomas and Elmy) conceived
Tommy's Margarita around 1987–88, replacing triple sec with agave nectar to let
100% agave tequila take center stage. In 2008 it became the first venue-specific
cocktail added to the IBA's official drinks list. The Mexican government
officially recognized Julio as Tequila Ambassador to North America. The Wall
Street Journal called it "the epicenter of tequila in the United States." Has
appeared on World's 50 Best Bars six times. A pilgrimage bar.
Lore rating: High. Tommy's Margarita is a genuine modern classic with a clean
attribution and a great family story behind it.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

CAFFÈ FLORIAN — Venice, Italy (est. 1720)
Signature drinks: Spritz, Bellini, Negroni (all served, none invented here)
The oldest continuously operating café in Italy. Opened December 29, 1720, by
Floriano Francesconi as "Alla Venezia Trionfante." Six ornate gilded rooms.
Casanova, Dickens, Hemingway, and countless others drank here. The Spritz was
born in its orbit — Austrian soldiers stationed in Venice during occupation
diluted wine with soda water; the habit spread. During WWII, waiters reportedly
served espresso by candlelight during air raids. Three centuries of gossip,
politics, and aperitivo in one room on the most famous square in the world.
Note: No single drink is cleanly attributed here; bar is context rather than
recipe origin. Valuable for Spritz and Venetian Aperitivo lore.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

BOADAS COCKTAILS — Barcelona, Spain (est. 1933)
Signature technique: The thrown Martini / Daiquiri
Barcelona's oldest cocktail bar, opened by Miguel Boadas who trained at El
Floridita in Havana. Bartenders in black jackets, white shirts, and bow ties
throw cocktails theatrically from one vessel to another through the air. The
drink is a Martini or Daiquiri; the technique is the invention. A direct living
link between pre-Prohibition Cuba and modern Europe. Revitalized in 2022 under
Simone Caporale (of acclaimed Sips, also Barcelona). Décor entirely unchanged.
Note: No single attributable recipe, but strong as context for Cuban technique
and its European diaspora.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

BAR BASSO — Milan, Italy (est. 1947)
Signature drink: Negroni Sbagliato
"Sbagliato" means mistaken. A bartender reached for Prosecco instead of gin
while making a Negroni, noticed only after pouring. The happy accident —
Prosecco, Campari, sweet vermouth — became a classic. Now a global staple and
a gateway drink for Negroni-curious drinkers who find the original too bitter.
Went viral in 2022 when actress Olivia Cooke ordered one on camera. That surge
is worth noting for lore; it has its own modern cultural moment layered on top
of the origin story.
Lore rating: High. Accident origin stories are among the best in cocktail lore.

================================================================================
NEW ORLEANS — A CITY OF ITS OWN
================================================================================

New Orleans deserves its own section. No city has produced more canonical
American cocktails per square block. The French Quarter alone contains:

NAPOLEON HOUSE (building 1797, bar 1914)
Signature drink: Pimm's Cup (American home)
A plot to rescue Napoleon from exile on St. Helena and house him in this building
was hatched by former New Orleans mayor Nicholas Girod in 1821. Napoleon died
before it could happen. The name stuck. The Pimm's Cup — a British import —
found its American home here in the late 1940s. Classical music, peeling plaster,
bust of Napoleon atop an antique cash register. Essentially unchanged in over
a century.

TUJAGUE'S (est. 1856)
Signature drink: Grasshopper
America's oldest stand-up bar — no stools, standing room only. The mirror behind
the bar was brought from Paris in 1856 and was already 90 years old on arrival,
making it now over 250 years old. Philip Guichet invented the Grasshopper in 1918
for a New York competition — crème de menthe, crème de cacao, cream, brandy —
came in second place, went home, served it at Tujague's ever after. During
Prohibition, waiters concealed bottles in their apron pockets. A Prohibition agent
claimed to have witnessed Philip Guichet serving absinthe; Guichet denied it.

LAFITTE'S BLACKSMITH SHOP (building est. 1722–1732)
Likely the oldest structure used as a bar in the United States. Purportedly used
by pirate-privateer Jean Lafitte as a cover for smuggling (the timeline is murky
but the legend is vivid). One of the only surviving Creole cottages in the French
Quarter. Lit almost entirely by candlelight. Reportedly haunted. The building
survived the great fires of 1788 and 1794.

PAT O'BRIEN'S (est. 1933 as legal bar)
Signature drink: Hurricane
During WWII, bar owners were forced to buy 50 cases of rum to obtain a single
case of whiskey. Pat O'Brien's team invented the Hurricane to work through
their surplus — passion fruit syrup, lemon juice, rum — served in a hurricane-
lamp-shaped glass. Also widely credited with inventing the dueling piano concept.

THE OLD ABSINTHE HOUSE (building early 1800s)
The Old Absinthe House Frappe was created by Cayetano Ferrer in 1874. Absinthe
was banned in the US from 1912 to 2007; the drink was unavailable for nearly a
century. Legend holds that Andrew Jackson met with Jean Lafitte here before the
Battle of New Orleans. Now fully operational again with legal absinthe.

================================================================================
THE JAPANESE INFLUENCE — AN UNDERAPPRECIATED THREAD
================================================================================

ANGEL'S SHARE — New York City (est. 1993, recently relocated)
Hidden behind a Japanese restaurant in the East Village. Manhattan's first taste
of the Tokyo cocktail bar style: formality, intimacy, meticulous technique —
measuring, stirring, and shaking with precision at a time the US bar industry was
in a widely acknowledged dark age. The house rules posted on the wall directly
influenced Sasha Petraske when he opened Milk & Honey. Angel's Share quietly
seeded the entire craft revival before anyone was paying attention.

BAR GOTO — New York City (est. 2015)
Kenta Goto spent seven years at Pegu Club before opening on the Lower East Side.
The Sakura Martini — sake, gin, maraschino, cherry blossom — became his defining
drink. A New York bar with Japanese soul: technique-forward, delicate, aromatic,
unhurried. Made Japanese-inflected cocktail culture accessible to a broader
American audience without flattening it. Note: Goto is living/active; creator
outreach protocol applies.

================================================================================
THE CONTEMPORARY LEGENDS
================================================================================

MILK & HONEY — New York City (est. 1999, closed 2020)
The bar that changed everything. Opened by Sasha Petraske on the Lower East Side.
Launched the careers of a generation of bartenders and the global proliferation
of speakeasy-style bars. Birthplace of: Penicillin (Sam Ross), Gold Rush (TJ
Siegal), Paper Plane (Sam Ross). The Penicillin — blended Scotch, lemon, ginger,
honey, Islay float — is one of the most-ordered modern classics in the world.

PEGU CLUB — New York City (est. 2005, closed 2020)
Audrey Saunders opened on West Houston Street and immediately set the national
standard. Emphasized gin in a world awash in flavored vodka. Trained the
bartenders who went on to open PDT and Death & Co. The Old Cuban (aged rum, mint,
lime, Angostura, Champagne) debuted here after originating at Bemelmans. The
Gin-Gin Mule and Little Italy were also born here. Closed during COVID; a genuine
loss. Saunders is one of the most important figures in the American craft revival.

BEMELMANS BAR, THE CARLYLE — New York City (est. 1947)
Art Deco, gold-leaf ceiling, Ludwig Bemelmans murals (the Madeline illustrator —
the only public space where his original work survives). Audrey Saunders debuted
the Old Cuban here in 2002 — aged rum, mint, lime, Angostura bitters, Champagne,
a Mojito through finishing school — before bringing it to Pegu Club. JFK,
Elizabeth Taylor, Paul McCartney were regulars. The most glamorously
unreconstructed bar in Manhattan.

DEATH & CO — New York City (est. New Year's Eve 2006, now NYC/Denver/LA)
Opened New Year's Eve 2006 in the East Village; changed the drinks world. The
Oaxaca Old-Fashioned (Phil Ward, 2007) — tequila/mezcal riff on the Old-Fashioned
template — is now on Death & Co merch. The Naked and Famous (Joaquín Simó, 2011)
— equal parts mezcal, Aperol, yellow Chartreuse, lime — is a modern canon entry.
Their book is the bestselling cocktail book of all time. Multiple NYC/LA/Denver
locations. A training ground for the best bartenders of the current era.

PDT (PLEASE DON'T TELL) — New York City (est. 2007)
Enter through a phone booth inside Crif Dogs. The Benton's Old-Fashioned —
bourbon fat-washed with Benton's bacon, maple syrup, Angostura bitters —
essentially invented the fat-washing technique as a cocktail tool. Sells roughly
150 a week; owner Jeff Bell calls it the drink that defines PDT's legacy. Also
has a Hong Kong location.

ATTABOY — New York City (est. 2013)
Occupies the space where Milk & Honey once was, run by two Milk & Honey
protégés: Sam Ross and Michael McIlroy. No menu — you describe what you want
and the bartender builds something. 28 seats, no reservations. Named Best Bar
in North America in 2022. The Penicillin is here, but every drink is bespoke.
The model has influenced bartender culture worldwide.

EMPLOYEES ONLY — New York City (est. 2004, now global)
Walk past a tarot card reader to get in. West Village stalwart since 2004. One
of the first craft revival bars to successfully expand internationally — Singapore,
LA, Miami, and beyond — without losing its identity. The Mata Hari (brandy,
Cointreau, apricot, blood orange, bitters) is a house signature.

THE DEAD RABBIT — New York City (est. 2013)
Founded by Belfast childhood friends Jack McGarry and Sean Muldoon. Ground floor
is a traditional Irish pub; second-floor Parlor is where the cocktails live. The
cocktail menu is a hand-illustrated graphic novel. Named World's Best Bar twice
(2015, 2016). Signature Irish Coffee — Teeling whiskey, Andytown coffee, freshly
whipped cream — is the modern benchmark for the drink. The name comes from a
real Irish gang that once roamed the same streets.

FLATIRON LOUNGE — New York City (est. 2003, closed 2018)
Julie Reiner's first bar. Did what Milk & Honey couldn't: bring craft cocktails
to the masses. No secret entrance, high volume, consistent quality. Trained Phil
Ward, Lynnette Marrero, Giuseppe González, and others who defined the next
generation. The Flatiron Martini — vodka, Cointreau, Lillet — was the drink of
the craft revival's first wave.

CLOVER CLUB — Brooklyn, New York (est. 2008)
Julie Reiner opened in Carroll Gardens when Brooklyn was still a gamble for
serious cocktails. Named for the pre-Prohibition classic: gin, raspberry syrup,
lemon juice, egg white. Named Best Cocktail Lounge one year after opening at
Tales of the Cocktail. The bar that brought craft culture to the outer boroughs
and proved it could sustain there.

DANTE — New York City (est. 1915 as café, relaunched 2015)
A Greenwich Village café for a century before relaunch as a cocktail destination.
Named World's Best Bar 2019. The Garibaldi — Campari, freshly whipped "fluffy"
orange juice — is deceptively simple; the texture transformation from aeration
is the technique. Probably the best Negroni in New York. Platform for the
aperitivo revival in America.

VIOLET HOUR — Chicago, IL (est. 2007)
One of the first serious craft cocktail bars outside New York. The Juliet & Romeo
(gin, lime, cucumber, sugar, mint, salt, rose water) was invented by Toby Maloney
who wanted a gin cocktail that would appeal to gin-haters. "I wanted it to taste
like a walk through an English garden." By 2019, the bar sold 20 a day; only
the Old-Fashioned outsells it. Brought the New York craft revival to Chicago
with its own identity.

MAISON PREMIERE — Brooklyn, New York (est. 2011)
Belle Époque New Orleans meets Brooklyn. The only operational vintage absinthe
fountain in America — a replica of the one at New Orleans' Old Absinthe House.
Bartenders in period dress, three-piece vintage suits. Demolished decades of
absinthe mythology. An extraordinary Ramos Gin Fizz with twelve ingredients.
James Beard Award winner. The bar that made absinthe a serious American spirit
again.

TRICK DOG — San Francisco, CA (est. 2013)
Mission District. The cocktail menu is redesigned twice yearly as a completely
different creative concept — Pantone swatches, astrology charts, Whole Earth
Catalog homages, and more. The menu itself is the invention. Regularly on World's
50 Best. The bar that gave San Francisco a distinct cocktail identity independent
of New York.

SMUGGLER'S COVE — San Francisco, CA (est. 2009)
The world's most serious Tiki bar. Owner Martin Cate also wrote the definitive
Tiki reference book. Largest rum selection in the United States. Where Don the
Beachcomber invented Tiki as escapism, Smuggler's Cove treats it as scholarship.
Named Best Bar in the US by Tales of the Cocktail in 2016; on World's 50 Best
six consecutive years.

CURE — New Orleans, USA (est. 2009)
The bar that brought craft cocktail culture to post-Katrina New Orleans — and
did it outside the French Quarter, which takes nerve. Still considered the place
to go in New Orleans for serious contemporary cocktails. Launched the careers of
a generation of New Orleans bartenders and elevated the city's international
cocktail reputation.

CONNAUGHT BAR — London, UK (est. 2008 in current form)
The Martini is served from a trolley. A selection of aromatic bitters to
customize; the bartender stirs tableside. The ceremony is the signature.
Consistently in World's 50 Best top five. The Dukes Martini for a new generation.
Has its own house-distilled gin.

HANDSHAKE SPEAKEASY — Mexico City, Mexico (est. 2020)
Hidden behind a secret door. Bar Director Eric van Beek melds tradition and
innovation via an on-site lab. Named World's Best Bar 2024 — the first Mexican
bar to hold the title. Signals a genuine shift in the center of gravity of global
cocktail culture.

PARADISO — Barcelona, Spain (est. 2017)
Enter through a hidden door in a pastrami sandwich fridge. Dalí-esque décor.
Named World's Best Bar 2022. Made Barcelona a serious cocktail destination.
The entry experience as part of the drink.

================================================================================
LORE NOTES & PATTERNS
================================================================================

THE HEMINGWAY THREAD
Hemingway is a direct lore connection across: El Floridita (bronze statue at
his stool), La Bodeguita (his endorsement quote), Harry's Bar Venice (regular),
Harry's New York Bar Paris (regular), Bar Hemingway Ritz Paris (liberation story),
Dukes Bar (Ian Fleming connection by extension). He is practically a through-line
for half the historic canon. Worth noting but not overusing.

THE WOMEN WHO BUILT THE BAR
Two stories stand out for their Singapore Sling resonance:
1. Ada Coleman (Hanky Panky, Savoy) — only female head bartender in the bar's
   history; credited by name in The Savoy Cocktail Book for exactly one drink.
2. Ngiam Tong Boon (Singapore Sling) — designed the drink specifically as cover
   for women to drink publicly in colonial Singapore.
3. Audrey Saunders (Old Cuban, Pegu Club, Flatiron) — most important woman in
   the American craft revival; trained a generation.
4. Julie Reiner (Clover Club, Flatiron Lounge) — democratized craft cocktails,
   opened Brooklyn's cocktail scene.

THE ACCIDENT ORIGIN STORIES
Some of the best lore in the canon involves drinks born from mistakes:
- Negroni Sbagliato: Prosecco grabbed instead of gin
- Bénédictine in the Vieux Carré: happy addition, not subtraction
Worth a thematic note in lore writing — accidents as invention.

THE DEGRADATION STORY
The Mai Tai is the canonical example of a great drink destroyed by popularity
and then rescued by scholars. The original 1944 recipe (rum-forward, barely
sweet) vs. the beach bar version (juice-heavy, sweet, unrecognizable) is a
genuine editorial story worth telling. The Zombie has a similar arc.

THE CODED RECIPES
Donn Beach encoded his Tiki recipes to prevent replication. Jeff "Beachbum"
Berry spent decades decoding them. This is the best story in the Tiki canon
and possibly one of the best stories in all of cocktail lore.
